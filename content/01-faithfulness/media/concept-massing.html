How you check whether an AI answer is actually backed up

An assistant answers a question about a property listing. Some of what it said is in the documents it read. Some of it isn't. Each panel is one move that gets you from a confident answer to a number you can defend. Gray is what earlier steps already built, blue is what this step adds, and red is what it takes away.

A confident answer, with a soft spot

The assistant answers fluently, and the answer arrives as one lump — you either accept the whole thing or you don't. One piece of it isn't in the documents it retrieved. Nothing in the system is currently looking for that.

The problem  ·  grounded question answering

Stand the answer up so it can be measured

Instead of judging the whole answer at once, treat it as a structure: break it into separate statements, check each one, then add up how many held. This gives you a shape to work with — it does not yet give you a number worth trusting.

From RAGAS  ·  the evaluation loop

Cut it down to single facts

“It has two bedrooms and a gym” looks like one statement but it is two, and only one of them might be true. If you check them together, half a mistake passes as a pass. Cut apart, each fact has to stand on its own.

From FActScore  ·  one fact at a time

Cut away whatever the documents don't back

Each fact is checked against the retrieved documents only — not against general knowledge. A fact can be true in the real world and still get cut here, because the system had no source for it. What survives, over what you started with, is the score.

The measurement  ·  supported over total

Point it at the failures that actually happen

A price that's wrong. Details from two different flats blended into one. A feature nobody mentioned. Nothing retrieved at all. Eight specific ways this goes wrong in a property assistant, all aimed at the same answer — and the checker has to survive every one.

From the domain  ·  not from any paper

Then check the checker

The thing doing the grading is itself a machine making judgement calls. So you grade it too: put its verdict next to a person's on the same cases and see whether they land in the same place. On the eight cases tested, they do. The red outline is everything not tested yet.
